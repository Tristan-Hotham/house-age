# Cover note: How Old Is Your Neighbourhood?

## Suggested journals (no author fee on the route proposed)

Checked on the journals' own author guidelines in 2026. Both are Sage subscription journals with optional paid open access (Sage Choice); submit on the standard subscription route, where "there are no fees payable to submit or publish in this journal". Recheck before submission.

1. **Urban Studies** (Sage). General Articles of 4,000 to 10,000 words; unstructured abstract of 150 to 200 words. Suits the vintage, recentralisation and neighbourhood-change framing. The current text (about 7,200 words) fits; the abstract (241 words) must be cut to 200.
2. **Environment and Planning B: Urban Analytics and City Science** (Sage). Original manuscripts of about 8,000 words, a limit that includes all text, references, figures and tables (each figure or table counts as roughly 250 to 500 words). Suits a complete small-area measurement with an open map and code, but this version (10 figures and 9 tables) is well over the limit and would need the appendix, several tables and figures moved to supplementary material. The journal's short "Urban Data/Code" format (up to 3,000 words) is an alternative for the data product alone.

Possible but not checked: **Area** (RGS-IBG, Wiley, subscription with optional open access); its word limit could not be confirmed in this review and a much shorter version would be needed.

Not suggested: **Buildings and Cities** (open access; charges an Article Publication Charge of £1,450 for research papers, with waivers prioritised for authors in least developed countries) and **Journal of Maps** (Taylor and Francis, fully open access; its APC could not be confirmed in this review, so it is not proposed).

Length of this version: about 7,200 words, 10 figures and 9 tables (including two in the appendix).

## Summary of the contribution

The Valuation Office Agency now publishes the build period of every property on the Council Tax lists for all 35,672 small areas (LSOAs) of England and Wales. This paper uses the 2025 counts to map the age of the housing stock at the finest public scale and to test, in a plan written before any distance was computed, whether the 34 cities of England and Wales hold their housing in rings of age as vintage models of urban growth predict. They do through the twentieth century: each successive period's homes, from before 1900 to 1965 to 1972, stand 4.3% farther from the centre (95% CI 3.1 to 5.5), a pattern no placebo centre reproduces. Then the order reverses: homes built since 2009 stand 9.0% closer to the centre than those of 1983 to 1999, in 26 of 34 cities, and are the largest group of homes in the first kilometre. A simple linear age gradient, the first pre-specified hypothesis, fails the pre-specified placebo test. Maps, data and code are released.
